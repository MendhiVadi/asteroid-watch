1.1 Conventions, frames, constants
----------------------------------
Units      length AU (1 AU = 149,597,870.7 km), time day (86,400 s), angles in radians inside the
           code and in degrees in the data files.
Frame      ecliptic J2000: x toward the J2000 vernal equinox, z toward ecliptic north, y completes the
           right-handed set. SBDB elements and the Horizons vectors we request (REF_PLANE=ECLIPTIC,
           REF_SYSTEM=ICRF) use it. "Heliocentric" = origin at the Sun, "barycentric" = origin at the
           solar-system barycentre, "geocentric" = origin at Earth's centre.
Time       Julian Date (JD) = a continuous day count. J2000 = JD 2451545.0. Unix ms -> JD:
           jd = ms / 86400000 + 2440587.5 (kepler.ts jdFromMs / msFromJd).
Scene axes three.js is Y-up. Ecliptic (x, y, z) maps to scene (x, z, -y): ecliptic north is scene +Y
           and the ecliptic plane is the scene XZ plane (kepler.ts offsetToScene and
           cinemaOffsetToScene; cinematic/ephemeris.ts toWide).
Constants  k = 0.01720209895 rad/day, the Gaussian gravitational constant (kepler.ts GAUSS_K,
           trajectory.py K_GAUSS). k^2 equals GM_sun in AU^3/day^2 to 5e-12 relative (measured).
           2*pi/k = 365.2568983 d. c = 299,792.458 km/s = 173.1446 AU/day (nbody/constants.ts).
           Earth radius 6371 km = 4.2588e-5 AU; Moon radius 1737.4 km = 1.1614e-5 AU.
           1 lunar distance (LD) = 0.00256955 AU = 384,399 km (kepler.ts LUNAR_DISTANCE_AU).

1.2 Orbital elements (what the data holds for each asteroid)
------------------------------------------------------------
If only the Sun pulled, an orbit would be a fixed ellipse. Six numbers fix it, plus a time stamp:
    a      semi-major axis, AU: half the long diameter of the ellipse (size)
    e      eccentricity, 0 <= e < 1: 0 is a circle, near 1 a thin sliver (shape)
    i      inclination, degrees: tilt of the orbit plane against the ecliptic
    Om     longitude of the ascending node (JSON key "om"), degrees: where the orbit climbs through
           the ecliptic, measured from the x axis
    w      argument of perihelion (key "w"), degrees: angle in the orbit plane from that node to the
           point closest to the Sun
    M0     mean anomaly at the epoch (key "ma"), degrees: where the body is on the ellipse at the
           epoch, as a uniformly growing angle (see 1.4)
    epoch  JD (TDB) at which the six values are exact (key "epoch"; 30,170 of 42,670 objects use
           2461200.5 = 2026-06-09, there are 4,340 distinct epochs) (measured)
Derived: perihelion q = a(1 - e), aphelion Q = a(1 + e), period P (1.3).
These are OSCULATING elements: the ellipse the body would follow if the Sun were the only attractor
from the epoch on. Real orbits drift because planets pull too (1.12). SBDB serves them; we ask for
full double precision because a flyby amplifies small errors (1.19, 2.12).

1.3 Mean motion and period
--------------------------
    n = k / a^(3/2)                  [rad/day]       Kepler's third law in Gaussian form
    P = 2*pi / n = 365.2569 * a^(3/2) [days]
Code: kepler.ts buildElementTable (slot 2 of each row); trajectory.py asteroid_positions and
scan_block (n = K_GAUSS / a**1.5); ui/format.ts fmtPeriod (365.256 * a^1.5). The SBDB "per" field
is NOT used (SBDB rounds it to 3 significant digits). The N-body side uses n = sqrt(mu / |a|^3) with
mu = GM_sun from the manifest (nbody/elements.ts), the same value to 5e-12.

1.4 Mean anomaly
----------------
Mean anomaly is the "clock angle" of the orbit: it grows at the constant rate n although the real
speed varies (fast near the Sun, slow far away).
    M(t) = M0 + n * (t - epoch)       radians, then wrapped into [-pi, pi]
Code: kepler.ts helioPosition (t[b+3] + t[b+2] * (jd - t[b+4])); trajectory.py asteroid_positions.
Wrapping matters only for precision: over decades M reaches hundreds of radians.

1.5 Kepler's equation and the Newton-Raphson solve
--------------------------------------------------
Position needs the eccentric anomaly E (an angle on the circle that encloses the ellipse), tied to M by
    M = E - e * sin(E)                                   (Kepler's equation)
E cannot be written in closed form, so we iterate. With f(E) = E - e sin E - M we have
f'(E) = 1 - e cos E >= 1 - e > 0, never zero for an ellipse. Newton-Raphson:
    E_{k+1} = E_k - (E_k - e sin E_k - M) / (1 - e cos E_k)
Near the answer each step roughly squares the error (quadratic convergence).
Browser, kepler.ts solveKepler:
    M wrapped to [-pi, pi]
    start  E_0 = M + e sin M            if e < 0.8     (first-order series, error O(e^2))
           E_0 = +pi or -pi (sign of M) if e >= 0.8    (safe start for very eccentric orbits)
    stop when |dE| < 1e-11, at most 30 iterations
    buildElementTable clamps e to [0, 0.9999] and a to >= 1e-3.
N-body side, nbody/elements.ts solveKeplerElliptic: same start values, stop at 1e-15, max 60
iterations. solveKeplerHyperbolic solves M = e sinh H - H for e > 1 (SBDB uses e > 1, a < 0 for
hyperbolic orbits; the shipped catalogue has none: validate.py checks 0 <= e < 1).
Python, trajectory.py solve_kepler: Halley's method (third order) instead of Newton:
    dE = f / (f' - 0.5 * f * f'' / f'),   f'' = e sin E
    start E_0 = M + 0.85 e sign(sin M) (Danby), tolerance 1e-13, max 60 iterations, vectorised.
    Tested on 200,000 random cases plus e = 0.999: max residual 4.4e-16 (measured).
Streaming scan, trajectory.py scan_block: the 5-day grid is regular, so E at the next grid point is
predicted from the current one by a second-order Taylor step in M,
    g = n*dt / (1 - e cos E);    E_next ~ E + g - 0.5 * e * sin(E) * g^2 / (1 - e cos E)
then exactly two Halley corrections (a step > 1e-6 flags a straggler that gets full iteration).
sin and cos of the corrected E come from a second-order update of the old sin/cos, not new trig
calls. Test: matches the generic path to 2e-13 AU (measured).
Typical cost: 3-4 iterations. Apophis in 1.19: step sizes 1.2e-2, 1.2e-5, 1.1e-11, 2e-16.

1.6 From the orbit plane to the ecliptic (rotations by w, i, Om)
----------------------------------------------------------------
In the orbit's own plane ("perifocal" frame: x_p toward perihelion, y_p 90 degrees ahead in the
direction of motion):
    x_p = a (cos E - e)
    y_p = b sin E,    b = a sqrt(1 - e^2)                 (semi-minor axis)
    r   = sqrt(x_p^2 + y_p^2) = a (1 - e cos E)
and for the N-body starting velocity (nbody/elements.ts elementsToState)
    vx_p = -a n sin E / (1 - e cos E),    vy_p = b n cos E / (1 - e cos E)
To reach ecliptic coordinates rotate by w about z, tilt by i about x, rotate by Om about z:
R = Rz(Om) Rx(i) Rz(w). The first two columns of R are unit vectors P (toward perihelion) and
Q (90 degrees ahead), computed once per object:
    P = ( cos w cos Om - sin w sin Om cos i,   cos w sin Om + sin w cos Om cos i,   sin w sin i )
    Q = (-sin w cos Om - cos w sin Om cos i,  -sin w sin Om + cos w cos Om cos i,   cos w sin i )
    position = x_p * P + y_p * Q,        velocity = vx_p * P + vy_p * Q
Code: kepler.ts buildElementTable and helioPosition; trajectory.py orientation_vectors and
kepler_position; nbody/elements.ts elementsToState. P and Q are orthonormal (tested to 1e-14).
Packed table row, stride 12 (kepler.ts ELEM_STRIDE): a, e, n, M0 (rad), epoch, b, Px, Py, Pz, Qx, Qy,
Qz. Per frame and object that leaves one Kepler solve plus a few multiplies.

1.7 Earth: Standish mean elements and JPL DE441
-----------------------------------------------
A close approach is about the Earth-asteroid distance, so Earth needs a position too. Sources:
(a) Standish, "Keplerian Elements for Approximate Positions of the Major Planets", Table 1 (valid
    1800-2050), Earth-Moon barycentre row, with T = (JD - 2451545.0) / 36525 (Julian centuries):
        a     = 1.00000261  + 0.00000562 T            AU
        e     = 0.01671123  - 0.00004392 T
        I     = -0.00001531 - 0.01294668 T            degrees
        L     = 100.46457166 + 35999.37244981 T       degrees (mean longitude)
        varpi = 102.93768193 + 0.32327364 T           degrees (longitude of perihelion)
        Om    = 0
    Then w = varpi - Om, M = L - varpi, and 1.5-1.6 give the position. Code: kepler.ts earthHelio
    (dashboard dots, InfoCard distance, orbit trail); trajectory.py earth_elements and
    earth_position_standish (fallback); cinematic/ephemeris.ts PLANETS (fallback and orbit lines).
    It is the barycentre of Earth+Moon, which sits ~4,671 km (3.1e-5 AU) from Earth's centre. Versus
    DE441 it differs by up to 1.4e-4 AU (~21,000 km) over 2026-2100 (measured, test_trajectory.py).
(b) JPL Horizons vectors (ephemeris DE441) of the Earth CENTRE minus the Sun, daily with velocities, in
    app/public/data/ephemeris/planets.bin, cubic-Hermite interpolated (1.13). trajectory.py
    earth_position uses this wherever the file covers the date (now the whole 2026-10-09..2100-12-31
    window), else (a). Earth source is recorded in the report and in asteroids_trajectories.json meta.
(c) nbody/ephemeris.ts serves the same tables to the force model and to the solar-system view.

1.8 Geocentric distance, the coarse scan, and refinement of the minima
----------------------------------------------------------------------
With r the asteroid's heliocentric position and R the Earth's,
    d = |r - R|,        d^2 = |r|^2 + |R|^2 - 2 r.R
trajectory.py scan_block uses r.R = x_p (P.R) + y_p (Q.R) and |r| = a - a e cos E, so no per-object
3-vectors are built: 42,670 objects x 5,424 times in 45 s (runtime line of trajectory_report.md).
Time grid   JD 2461322.5 (2026-10-09) plus multiples of 5 d, plus the end point JD 2488433.5
            (2100-12-31): 27,111 d, 5,424 samples (make_grid).
Candidates  every grid local minimum with d < 0.3 AU, the two window-edge samples if they are minima,
            and each object's global grid minimum.
Refinement  (refine_candidates) three 21-point grids around each candidate with half-widths 5.0, 0.6,
            0.06 d (spacings 0.5, 0.06, 0.006 d), each centred on the previous minimum; then the vertex
            of a parabola through d^2 at three neighbouring points (spacing h = 0.006 d):
                t* = t_j + (h/2) (y_{j-1} - y_{j+1}) / (y_{j-1} - 2 y_j + y_{j+1}),     y = d^2
            accepted only if the vertex is interior, curvature positive, |t* - t_j| <= h, and d
            does not get larger.
Close approach  a refined minimum with d < 0.05 AU strictly inside the window; minima of one object
            within 2 d are merged (DEDUPE_DAYS). The first 3 per object ship as next_close_approach;
            n_close_approaches ships only when there are more than 3.
Why refine: Apophis' coarse 5-day minimum is 0.00662 AU, the refined one 0.000138 AU (measured).
Tests: the pipeline's close approaches equal a brute-force 0.5-day scan on 30 low-MOID objects (20
events, 0 missed, 0 extra), and a synthetic circular orbit (a = 1.03 AU) reproduces the analytic
conjunction minima to 1e-6 AU and 0.02 d (measured, test_trajectory.py).

1.9 MOID
--------
MOID = Minimum Orbit Intersection Distance: the least distance between the asteroid's ellipse and
Earth's ellipse, ignoring where each body is on its orbit. A tiny MOID makes an impact geometrically
possible; whether it happens also depends on timing.
The category rules use SBDB's MOID (key "moid" in neo_elements.json, AU; unknown for 134 objects).
trajectory.py also has orbit_moid, used only by tests as an independent check:
    sample both ellipses at 1500 equal steps of eccentric anomaly -> 1500 x 1500 distance matrix ->
    argmin -> 4 rounds of local 41 x 41 grids over +-2 steps, each round shrinking the step 10x.
    Earth's ellipse = Standish elements frozen at one date.
Agreement with SBDB (measured): Bennu 0.00292 vs 0.003223 AU; Apophis 0.000071 vs 0.000108 AU. The
few-1e-4 AU gaps (frozen Earth ellipse, coarse search) are why the pipeline trusts SBDB's value.

1.10 Diameter from absolute magnitude H
---------------------------------------
H is the brightness an asteroid would have at 1 AU from Sun and observer at zero phase angle. A shiny
surface (high albedo p) is smaller for the same H:
    D = 1329 / sqrt(p) * 10^(-H/5)          [km]     p = geometric albedo, H = absolute magnitude
Code: build_elements.py h_to_d and trajectory.py estimate_diameter, both with p = 0.14, used ONLY when
SBDB has no measured diameter. Fallback chain in build_elements.py: SBDB diameter, else the H formula,
else the mean of the CSV's est_diameter_min/max, else none (6 objects; trajectory.py then ships 0.05
km). Source counts (file): SBDB 1,245; H formula 41,419; none 6. So ~97% of the diameters on screen
are estimates, good to roughly a factor 2.
Apophis: H = 19.09. With p = 0.14: 3551.9 * 10^(-3.818) = 0.540 km. SBDB's measured albedo is 0.35:
1329 / sqrt(0.35) * 1.5206e-4 = 0.342 km, matching SBDB's 0.34 km (the shipped value).
Dashboard marker size (data.ts markerSize), scene units:  size = 0.034 + 0.058 * log10(1 + 100 * D_km),
e.g. 0.34 km -> 0.1236; 37.7 km (Ganymed, the largest) -> 0.2414.

1.11 Category rules (trajectory.py categorise): exactly one category per object
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Inputs per object: SBDB MOID (AU or unknown); PHA flag (SBDB null counts as false); min_dist = the
refined global minimum two-body geocentric distance over 2026-10-09..2100-12-31 (AU); and
    trend_10y  = d(START + 3650 d) - d(START), rounded to 4 decimals (shipped as trend_au_10y and
                 as the alias trend_au)
    trend_full = d(2100-12-31) - d(START), shipped for reference only (trend_au_full)
Thresholds: IMPACT_AU = 0.05 AU (7.48 million km = 19.5 LD), STRICT_AU = 0.001 AU (149,598 km = 0.39 LD).
    orbit_ok         = (MOID unknown) OR (MOID <= 0.05) OR PHA
    impact           = orbit_ok AND min_dist < 0.05
    strict_collision = impact AND min_dist < 0.001         (a flag, subset of impact)
    receding         = (NOT orbit_ok) AND trend_10y >= 0
    approaching      = everything else
In words: "impact" is short for impact-capable: the orbit can reach Earth's orbit region and the
two-body model passes within 0.05 AU on some date in the window. It does NOT say the object will hit.
"receding": the orbit stays clear of Earth's (MOID > 0.05 AU, not hazardous) and the distance does not
shrink over the first 10 years. "approaching": the rest (closing but safe, or a low-MOID orbit that never
comes within 0.05 AU in the window). The 10-year trend is deliberate: across 74 years d(end) - d(start)
compares two arbitrary orbital phases and its sign disagrees with the 10-year sign for 34.5% of objects
(trajectory_report.md). Edge rules (unit-tested on 12 hand-made cases): min_dist must be strictly
below 0.05; a trend of exactly 0 counts as receding; MOID exactly 0.05 is inside the gate.
Result (file, 42,670 objects): impact 12,314 (28.9%), approaching 20,540 (48.1%), receding 9,816
(23.0%); strict_collision 16; PHAs 2,550, none receding; objects outside the MOID/PHA gate that still
come within 0.05 AU: 0. Of 23,192 objects passing the orbit gate, 53.1% come within 0.05 AU at some time
in 74 years but only 10.0% (2,309) within the first 10 years. UI labels (lib/colors.ts):
"Impact-capable (diminishing radius)", "Approaching (closing, no impact path)", "Receding (never crash)".

1.12 N-body equation of motion (nbody/dynamics.ts makeForce)
------------------------------------------------------------
The selected asteroid is a massless test particle (it pulls on nothing) in the barycentric ecliptic
J2000 frame:
    d2x/dt2 = SUM_k  GM_k (x_k(t) - x) / |x_k(t) - x|^3   +   a_1PN
summed over ten point masses (nbody/ephemeris.ts BODY_ORDER): Sun, Mercury, Venus, Earth, Moon, Mars,
Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune. x_k(t) comes from the Hermite ephemeris (1.13). Mars..Neptune are
system barycentres with GM including their moons. Earth and Moon are SEPARATE bodies (Moon = Earth +
geocentric Moon from moon.bin), which matters for passes within a few hundred thousand km. GM from
the JPL DE440 header (manifest "gm"), AU^3/day^2 / km^3/s^2:
    Sun 2.9591221e-4 / 132,712,440,041   Earth 8.8877e-10 / 398,600.44   Moon 1.0932e-11 / 4,902.80
    Jupiter 2.8253e-7 / 126,712,764   Saturn 8.4597e-8 / 37,940,585   Uranus 1.2920e-8 / 5,794,556
    Neptune 1.5244e-8 / 6,836,527   Venus 7.2435e-10 / 324,858.6   Mars 9.5495e-11 / 42,828.4
    Mercury 4.9125e-11 / 22,031.9
Relativity: the Sun's first post-Newtonian correction (Schwarzschild, beta = gamma = 1). With
r = x - x_sun, u = v - v_sun, mu = GM_sun:
    a_1PN = mu / (c^2 r^3) * [ (4 mu / r - |u|^2) r_vec + 4 (r_vec . u) u ]
GM_sun/c^2 = 1.4766 km. The relative size is ~(v/c)^2 ~ 1e-8: it shows up as slow perihelion drift
over decades, not in one flyby. Option relativity: false switches it off.
Rounding control: the ephemeris supplies each body as base (table node) + corr (interpolation increment)
and the force forms (base - x_ref) + (corr - dx) (ephemeris.ts positionsSplit; ias15.ts passes x as
x_ref + dx). Subtracting two ~1 AU numbers for a particle 1e-4 AU from Earth would otherwise lose
~1e-16 AU, a visible noise floor in the force.
NOT modelled: Ceres, Vesta, Pallas and the other massive asteroids (JPL uses 16), Pluto, Yarkovsky and
other non-gravitational forces, radiation pressure, Earth oblateness (J2) and tides, solar J2, 1PN terms
of the planets. See 2.12.

1.13 Ephemeris tables and cubic Hermite interpolation
-----------------------------------------------------
Horizons gives positions AND velocities at nodes. Between nodes t_i and t_i + h, with s = (t - t_i)/h in
[0, 1], the cubic Hermite interpolant is
    p(s) = h00 p0 + h10 h v0 + h01 p1 + h11 h v1
    h00 = 2s^3 - 3s^2 + 1      h10 = s^3 - 2s^2 + s       h01 = -2s^3 + 3s^2      h11 = s^3 - s^2
and its derivative gives the velocity
    v(s) = ((6s^2 - 6s)/h) p0 + (3s^2 - 4s + 1) v0 + ((-6s^2 + 6s)/h) p1 + (3s^2 - 2s) v1
Position and velocity are exact at the nodes and continuous between them. Code: nbody/ephemeris.ts
(hermite6, positions, positionsSplit, moonRelState); trajectory.py _hermite (Earth only);
scripts/fetch_ephemeris.py hermite (validation).
Node spacing: planets 1 day; Moon (geocentric) 12 hours. Measured error versus fresh Horizons samples
(manifest "validation"): Earth worst 0.098 km, Mercury worst 20.0 km (fast orbit, 1-day nodes), Moon
worst 0.316 km, rms 0.122 km. At 4 test epochs (2029, 2036/37 seam, 2095, 2100-12-30) planets are
within 52 m and the Moon within 279 m of Horizons (measured, test_nbody.mts). Why 12 h: the coarsest
divisor of a day with Moon error below 1 km (12 h: 0.3 km, 24 h: 5.1 km, fetch_ephemeris.py docstring).

1.14 IAS15 integrator (nbody/ias15.ts)
--------------------------------------
IAS15 (Rein & Spiegel 2015) is an adaptive 15th-order integrator for y'' = a(t, y, y'). Over one step
dt it models the acceleration as a degree-7 polynomial in tau = (t - t0)/dt, tau in [0, 1]:
    a(tau) = a0 + b1 tau + b2 tau^2 + ... + b7 tau^7                 (b_p are 3-vectors; code: beta)
Integrating twice gives closed forms for velocity and position at any tau:
    v(tau) = v0 + dt ( a0 tau + SUM_p b_p tau^(p+1) / (p+1) )
    x(tau) = x0 + dt tau v0 + dt^2 ( a0 tau^2 / 2 + SUM_p b_p tau^(p+2) / ((p+1)(p+2)) )
x is a degree-9 polynomial; that is the "dense output" (class StepPoly) used for events and sampling.
Nodes: the polynomial is pinned by accelerations at 7 special times in the step, the Gauss-Radau nodes
(roots of P7(x) + P8(x), Legendre polynomials, root x = -1 dropped, mapped to [0,1]); computeNodes finds
them numerically when the module loads (nothing hard-coded). With h_0 = 0 at the step start:
    0, 0.0562625605, 0.1802406917, 0.3526247171, 0.5471536263, 0.7342101772, 0.8853209468, 0.9775206136
Newton form:  a(tau) = a0 + SUM_{k=1..7} g_k * PROD_{i=0..k-1} (tau - h_i)
g_k are divided differences of the node accelerations (gFromAccels) and are converted to the b_p (NK).
One step (IAS15.step):
 1. Predict. Node accelerations start from the previous step's polynomial re-expressed on the new step
    length (BINOM rescaling); the first step uses a0 at every node.
 2. Correct. Sweep nodes j = 1..7 (Gauss-Seidel): build x_j, v_j from the current b_p, evaluate the force
    there, recompute g_j, update b_p. Repeat until the change of g_7 relative to max|a| is < 1e-16, or
    stalls, max 12 sweeps.
 3. Error estimate. err = max|g_7| / max|a0|: the size of the highest-order term.
 4. Step control. dt_new = dt * (eps_eff / err)^(1/7) with eps = 1e-9 (REBOUND's default). If
    dt_new / dt < 0.25 the step is rejected and retried smaller; an accepted step may grow by at most 4x.
    Bounds in sim.ts runs: minDt 1e-9 d (class default), maxDt 20 d.
 5. Accept with compensated (Kahan) summation: cx, cv carry the low-order bits lost in each addition, so
    rounding does not pile up as a secular drift over thousands of steps. A step can be clipped to land
    exactly on a time (tLimit) and capped from outside (dtCap). sim.ts stepCap limits dt <= 0.2 * d / v
    for both Earth and Moon so a step cannot jump through a body.
Noise-floor guard: close to a body, force rounding noise does not shrink with dt, so err stops falling and a
plain controller would crawl at minDt. The code keeps floorErr: if an accepted smaller step (dt < 0.9 *
previous dt) leaves err > eps and err > 0.4 * previous err, then floorErr = max(floorErr, err); floorErr
decays 0.98 per step; and eps_eff = min(max(eps, 2 * floorErr), 1e-6).
Measured (test_nbody.mts): an e = 0.5 two-body orbit over 100 periods keeps |dE/E| < 7.4e-15 and
|dL/L| < 1.3e-15, returns to its start within 1.2e-11 AU in 15,366 steps, and forward-then-backward
integration returns within 7e-12 AU (time-reversible); Apophis at eps = 1e-9 versus 1e-12 agrees to
4e-14 AU just after the flyby.

1.15 Elements -> state vector, and how a run starts
---------------------------------------------------
nbody/elements.ts elementsToState turns (a, e, i, Om, w, M0) into a heliocentric position and velocity
at the epoch: solve Kepler (1.5), perifocal position and velocity (1.6), rotate with P and Q, with
mu = GM_sun. The integrator is barycentric, so sim.ts initialState adds the Sun's barycentric state from
the ephemeris at the epoch: x_bary = x_helio + x_sun, v_bary = v_helio + v_sun. The state is then
integrated silently (no samples) from the epoch to the requested start date, forward or backward. If
the epoch lies outside the tables, the elements are first moved by Sun-only two-body motion
(propagateElementsTwoBody: only M changes, M += n dt) to 1 day inside the nearest edge; twoBodyDays
reports how far. The reverse map stateToElements (h = r x v; e-vector = ((v^2 - mu/r) r - (r.v) v) / mu;
a = -mu / (2 (v^2/2 - mu/r)); ...) is used only by tests (round trip good to 1e-12 in a and e).
Saved samples: heliocentric = barycentric - Sun; geocentric = barycentric - Earth (sim.ts writeSample).

1.16 Time scales: TDB versus UTC
--------------------------------
SBDB epochs and Horizons tables are in TDB (Barycentric Dynamical Time); clocks run on UTC.
    TDB ~ TT (differ < 2 ms),    TT = UTC + 69.184 s        (37 leap seconds + 32.184 s)
    JD_TDB = (unix_ms + 69,184) / 86,400,000 + 2440587.5
Code: nbody/constants.ts jdTdbFromUnixMs and unixMsFromJdTdb (TT_MINUS_UTC_S = 69.184, valid from 2017
until the next leap second; no future leap-second table); cinematic/controller.ts toTdb and fromTdb. The
cinematic HUD prints UTC through the inverse. Elsewhere the 69 s is ignored: the dashboard clock is a UTC
JD fed straight into the Kepler formulas (position effect ~ n * 69 s ~ 1e-5 rad, invisible) and Python's
jd_to_iso labels TDB Julian dates with a "Z".

1.17 Events: close approach, impact, Hill sphere, escape energy, look-ahead
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
All in sim.ts simulateStream. After each accepted step the step polynomial gives x(tau), v(tau) anywhere
in the step and the ephemeris gives Earth and Moon at the same time, so distances can be tested between
steps without taking more steps. For each body (Earth, Moon): d(tau) = |x - x_body| and
    H(tau) = dt * (rel_pos . rel_vel)             sign of d(d)/dt, direction-aware
d has a minimum where H changes from negative to non-negative.
Scan: each step is cut into NSCAN = 16 sub-intervals. A step is skipped if it cannot come close: the
least distance is at least (d0 + d1 - L)/2 with L = 1.5 * |dt| * speed bounding the path length; if that
exceeds 1.2 * max(0.05, 0.01, 0.0135) = 0.06 AU, nothing is computed.
Root finding: bisection on the step polynomial; 55 halvings for a minimum (root of H) and 60 for the
crossing of a radius (root of d - R); the result is far below a metre.
Rules:
  close approach  a distance minimum below 0.05 AU (Earth) or 0.01 AU (Moon) is recorded with distance,
                  date and relative speed.
  impact          d < R_body, R_earth = 6371 km, R_moon = 1737.4 km. The crossing time is found by
                  bisection on d - R and the run ENDS there (status impact_earth / impact_moon), last
                  sample on the surface. No atmosphere, no breakup.
  Hill sphere     a fixed radius 0.01 AU = 1.496 million km around Earth (HILL_RADIUS_AU) marks hill_entry
                  and hill_exit events. The physical Hill radius for Earth+Moon,
                  a (m/3M)^(1/3), is 0.01004 AU (measured arithmetic).
  escape energy   at a Hill exit, with r, v the geocentric state and mu_EM = GM_Earth + GM_Moon:
                      energy = |v|^2 / 2 - mu_EM / |r|           (specific two-body energy)
                  a candidate for escape needs energy > 0 (unbound) AND r.v > 0 (receding). At 0.01 AU
                  the escape speed sqrt(2 mu_EM / r) is only 0.734 km/s, so nearly everything leaving
                  the sphere is "unbound"; that alone is not trusted:
  look-ahead      the Sun and planets can pull such an object back (it leaves Earth on a hyperbola but
                  stays on an Earth-like solar orbit). lookaheadReturn forks a second integration from the
                  exit state, same force model and tolerance, up to the end of the tables. "Returns" if,
                  after first getting beyond 0.05 AU (RETURN_RADIUS_AU), it re-enters 0.05 AU, or if it
                  never gets beyond it. Only if it never returns is the verdict escaped: the main run
                  then keeps sampling postEscapeDays = 10 more days and stops (status escaped);
                  otherwise "continues". Tested against a brute-force re-entry check.
  requireEncounter (default true) the escape test fires only after the object has been inside the Hill
                  sphere (or started inside); otherwise every NEO would be "escaped" at t0.
  window end      the run reaches the end of the tables (or the safety caps maxSteps 3,000,000 /
                  maxSamples 600,000, flagged truncated).
Sampling tiers (DEFAULT_SAMPLING): every 1 minute inside 0.01 AU of Earth, hourly inside 0.05 AU, daily
beyond. The Apophis run: 7,472 of 7,472 inside-Hill sample gaps are 1 minute (measured).

1.18 Compressed radial scales used for rendering
------------------------------------------------
Real sizes do not fit one picture: asteroids sit 1e-4 to ~6 AU from Earth while Earth's radius is 4.3e-5 AU
(1 AU = ~23,500 Earth radii). Three monotone maps turn a real distance r (AU) from a centre into a scene
radius and keep the direction, so "nearer in reality" stays "nearer on screen". Scene unit: Earth radius = 2.
(a) Dashboard (kepler.ts compressRadius, used by offsetToScene on the Earth-to-asteroid vector):
        R(r) = R0 + K * log10(1 + r / S),      R0 = 3.4,  K = 5.6,  S = 0.001 AU
    r -> 0: 3.4 (just outside the 2.16 atmosphere shell); r = 0.000138 AU: 3.71; 1 LD: 6.49 (this is the
    Moon's drawn orbit, MOON_ORBIT_SCENE); 0.05 AU: 12.96; 1 AU: 20.2. Sizes are NOT to scale.
(b) Cinematic Earth view (kepler.ts cinemaRadius, EARTH_RADIUS_AU = 4.2635e-5), true scale out to 6 Earth
    radii, logarithmic beyond:
        R(r) = r * (2 / EARTH_RADIUS_AU)                              for r <= 6 R_E = 2.558e-4 AU
        R(r) = 12 + 12 * ln( r / (6 R_E) )                            for r > 6 R_E
    Continuous at R = 12. An impact reaches the globe surface (R = 2 at r = R_E); Apophis' 2029 pass at
    0.000254 AU draws at 11.9, the Moon at 39.7, 1 AU at 111.
(c) Solar-system view (cinematic/ephemeris.ts wideRadius, used by toWide on HELIOCENTRIC vectors):
        R(r) = 14 * ln(1 + r / 0.35)
    Mercury 10.4, Earth 18.9, Jupiter 38.7, Neptune 62.5. Planet sizes are not to scale.
Cinematic mode blends (b) and (c) with a weight w in [0, 1] (2.11).

2.2 Step 0 - audit of the CSV files (repo root)
-----------------------------------------------
neo_v2.csv columns: id, name, est_diameter_min, est_diameter_max, relative_velocity, miss_distance,
orbiting_body, sentry_object, absolute_magnitude, hazardous. 90,836 data rows, 27,423 unique (id, name)
pairs (measured). neo_cleaned.csv has the same 90,836 rows without id and name, so it cannot be joined to
anything; only neo_v2.csv is used. The CSV has no orbital elements, no MOID and no date column: it is a list
of observed close approaches. Columns used: miss_distance (km), hazardous, est_diameter_min/max (km); the
unit of relative_velocity is not stated in the file and it is unused.
Does it cover all asteroids? No: it reaches 27,181 of 42,670 SBDB NEOs (63.7%); 15,489 (36.3%, 422 of
them PHAs) are absent (coverage_report.md). That is why the SBDB catalogue is fetched.

2.3 Step 1 - JPL SBDB fetch (scripts/fetch_sbdb.py)
---------------------------------------------------
One HTTPS GET to https://ssd-api.jpl.nasa.gov/sbdb_query.api with sb-kind=a (asteroids), sb-group=neo,
full-prec=true and fields
    spkid, full_name, pdes, name, a, e, i, om, w, ma, epoch, per, moid, H, diameter, albedo, pha, neo
full-prec=true returns full double precision; the default rounds elements, which ruins flyby prediction
(1.19, 2.12). No pagination was needed: one reply of 10,028,018 bytes, count = 42,670. Up to 6 attempts with
growing back-off; exit code 2 on network failure. data/raw/sbdb_neo.json is the raw reply: signature,
fields[], data[] (rows of STRINGS such as "1.458243716760167") and count.

2.4 Step 2 - matching CSV ids to SBDB, writing neo_elements.json (scripts/build_elements.py)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
CSV ids are old-style SPK ids (2,000,000 + number for numbered asteroids), SBDB uses 20,000,000 + number,
so direct matching works for only some. Tried per CSV object, in this order (counts from the report):
    spkid equality                          6,259
    leading number in name -> SBDB pdes     2,727     "162635 (2000 SS164)" -> 162635
    parenthesised designation -> pdes      17,557
    designation -> full_name                  799
    SBDB single-object API alias lookup        37     (sstr=key; cached in data/raw/sbdb_alias_resolve.json)
    unmatched                                  44     (36 confirmed by SBDB as no longer NEO-class)
27,379 of 27,423 CSV objects (99.8%) matched, to 27,181 distinct SBDB objects (198 SBDB objects appear
under more than one CSV id: renumbered or duplicated designations). Then ONE ROW PER SBDB NEO is written:
nothing was dropped (no null elements, no e >= 1 or a <= 0). Fields: elements, per, moid, H, diameter_km
and diameter_src (sbdb | H_albedo0.14 | csv_mean | none), pha (Y -> true; null -> false, 134 nulls), id =
SBDB spkid as a string, name = SBDB full_name, and CSV extras (csv_id, in_csv, hazardous_csv,
min_miss_distance_km_csv). validate.py re-counts the filter independently on the raw rows and checks
types, ranges, uniqueness and in_csv consistency (all pass, measured).

2.5 Step 3 - JPL Horizons ephemeris tables (scripts/fetch_ephemeris.py)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
Why: the N-body engine and trajectory.py's Earth need accurate Sun, planet, Earth and Moon states.
Horizons API https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/api/horizons.api, EPHEM_TYPE=VECTORS, VEC_TABLE=2 (position +
velocity), OUT_UNITS=AU-D, REF_PLANE=ECLIPTIC, REF_SYSTEM=ICRF, CSV rows:
    bodies  Sun (10), Mercury (199), Venus (299), Earth (399) and the system barycentres of Mars (4),
            Jupiter (5), Saturn (6), Uranus (7), Neptune (8); centre = solar-system barycentre "500@0"
    Moon    (301) centred on Earth "500@399" (geocentric)
    grid    planets every 1 day, Moon every 12 h, 2020-01-01 00:00 TDB .. 2100-12-31 00:00 TDB inclusive:
            29,585 daily rows (JD 2458849.5..2488433.5) and 59,169 Moon rows
GM values come from the JPL DE440 header (ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/ftp/eph/planets/ascii/de440/header.440), with
Earth and Moon split from the Earth-Moon GM using EMRAT = 81.30056822. Positions are labelled DE441
(Horizons), constants DE440; at this accuracy the mix is harmless.
Mechanics: requests are chunked (3,100 planet rows, 4,400 Moon rows) and run in a thread pool (--workers,
default 4; Horizons answers 503 above ~4-6), with exponential back-off and jitter; every reply is checked
(the $$SOE marker, row count, JD grid). --cache-dir stores finished chunks so a failed run resumes. If
Horizons or the GM source is unreachable the script exits 2 and writes nothing; it never fabricates data.
Extending: --extend keeps the existing tables, fetches only the missing span starting from the LAST
existing row, requires that overlap row to match the disk (< 1e-9 AU, < 1e-10 AU/day), appends, and re-grids
the Moon by pure subsampling (every 6th 2-hour node = 12 h). The manifest records
extension {extendedUtc, baseEndJd 2465058.5, seamIndex 6209}. The original tables (made 2026-10-09) ended
2036-12-31 with a 2-hour Moon grid; they were extended to 2100-12-31 the same day (manifest "extension").
--validate / --validate-only compare Hermite interpolation with fresh Horizons samples (Earth, Mercury,
Moon; windows at the start, the seam, the middle, the end) and store the errors in manifest "validation"
(1.13). Sizes (measured): planets.bin 12,780,720 B, moon.bin 2,840,112 B, manifest.json 3,297 B.

2.7 Two tiers: two-body screening for everything, N-body for one, and why
-------------------------------------------------------------------------
Tier 1, two-body (Keplerian), all 42,670 objects. Closed form: the position at any time is one Kepler solve and
a rotation, independent of history. Used twice: offline in Python for categories and minima (5,424 samples x
42,670 objects in under a minute) and live in the browser for the dots (every frame, any clock speed, running
backwards, no stored state). Limit: only the Sun pulls, so planetary perturbations, Earth's pull during a flyby
and non-gravitational forces are absent.
Tier 2, N-body, the one selected object. Numerical integration through the real force field (10 bodies + 1PN)
with event detection. A 74-year run is ~0.1 s in JavaScript (Apophis: 79 ms and 150 ms in two runs, measured). It
finds impacts, Moon passes and escape, and reproduces Horizons to ~2 km for Apophis. Cost: ~3,000-4,000 steps,
each with 8 or more evaluations of a 10-body force plus ephemeris interpolation.
Why not N-body for all: 42,670 runs at ~0.1 s is roughly an hour of single-thread work (a rough extrapolation,
not measured), impossible per frame in a browser, and unnecessary for an overview where position and colour
are what matter. Why not two-body for the selected one: it cannot show impacts, Moon passes or escape, and it
is off by a factor 1.8 for Apophis (0.000138 versus 0.000254 AU).
The tiers also trust the elements differently: tier 1 treats them as an ellipse around the Sun; tier 2 converts
them to a state vector at the epoch (1.15) and lets the planets act on it. For 5,297 objects (12.4%) the element
epoch is before 2020-01-01, outside the ephemeris; for them tier 2 first moves the elements to 2020-01-02 by
Sun-only two-body motion (twoBodyDays), so their N-body start state is less accurate (Apophis from a 1987 epoch:
0.00414 AU off, measured in test_nbody.mts).

2.8 Updating ~42k asteroids every frame (lib/positions.ts, lib/keplerWorker.ts, lib/kepler.ts)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Load (lib/data.ts): fetch /data/asteroids.json (cache: no-cache), parse, drop invalid rows (unknown category,
a <= 0, e outside [0, 1), non-finite elements), then build typed arrays:
    table     Float64Array(count * 12)   packed per-object constants (1.6), 4.1 MB
    cat       Uint8Array(count)          0 impact, 1 approaching, 2 receding
    size      Float32Array(count)        marker scale (1.10)
    haystack  string[]                   lower-case "name id" for search
    counts and stats (PHA total, closest pass, largest)
If the real file fails to load, /data/asteroids.mock.json (random placeholder orbits from app/scripts/gen-mock.mjs,
names ending "(mock)") is used and the hero shows a "Demo data" badge.
Per frame (scene/AsteroidField.tsx useFrame) calls engine.update(clock.jd) on a PositionEngine:
  - A module Web Worker (keplerWorker.ts) holds a copy of the table. The main thread posts {step, jd, buf}; the
    worker runs computeScenePositions: earthHelio(jd) once, then for every object helioPosition, subtract Earth,
    offsetToScene (1.18a), writing xyz into buf, and posts buf back.
  - Buffers are TRANSFERRED (postMessage transfer list), not copied. Two Float32Array(count * 3) (512 KB each)
    ping-pong, "double buffering": the render loop reads `current` while the worker fills `spare`; when a reply
    arrives they swap. At most one request is in flight and nothing is posted if jd is unchanged (paused). So the
    main thread never waits for 42k Kepler solves; the dots may lag the clock by a frame or two.
  - If the worker fails to start or errors, PositionEngine falls back to inline computation. start() and dispose()
    are idempotent and restartable, so React StrictMode's mount-unmount-mount keeps a single worker.
Simulation clock: clock.jd (lib/store.ts) is a plain mutable object, not React state. CameraRig's ClockDriver adds
speed * delta each frame, speed from SPEEDS = [-100, -30, -10, -3, -1, 0, 0.25, 1, 3, 10, 30, 100] simulated days
per real second (default 1), clamped to [today, end of ephemeris coverage]. Users with prefers-reduced-motion start paused.
Frame rate was not benchmarked for this file.

2.9 InstancedMesh, colouring, picking (scene/AsteroidField.tsx, scene/rockGeometry.ts, scene/OrbitTrail.tsx)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- One THREE.InstancedMesh draws all visible asteroids in a single draw call. Geometry: an icosahedron whose
  vertices are pushed in or out by a smooth function of position and squashed into an irregular lump
  (makeRockGeometry); detail 0 (20 triangles) when count > 12,000. Material: MeshStandardMaterial, flat shading,
  roughness 0.85, patched in onBeforeCompile to add 0.38 x its own colour as emission so category colours stay
  readable on the night side. frustumCulled is off and raycast disabled.
- Each frame the CPU writes every instance matrix: a rotation about a per-object random axis (axis, phase, rate
  from a seeded LCG, fixed per session), uniform scale size[k] * boost, translation from the position buffer.
  boost = clamp(cameraDistance / 30, 1, 2.5) keeps far markers legible. The selected asteroid gets scale 0 in the
  instanced mesh and is drawn as a separate detailed rock (icosahedron detail 3) plus a camera-facing ring.
- Visibility: the toggles build a Uint32Array of visible indices; mesh.count = its length. Colours: per-instance
  instanceColor from CATEGORY_RGB (impact red #ff4d5a, approaching amber #ffb020, receding cyan #3cd6f0),
  rewritten only when the visible set changes.
- Picking is in screen space, not by raycasting 42k meshes (hundreds of ms per click versus < 1 ms): project each
  visible position with the view-projection matrix, keep those within max(11 px, size * boost * focal / w + 5),
  score = (pixel distance / threshold)^2 + w * 1e-4 (closest to the cursor, then nearest the camera), and drop
  anything hidden behind the Earth sphere (ray-sphere test). A click counts if the pointer moved <= 5 px in < 700
  ms; hover is throttled to one pick per animation frame and feeds the Tooltip. Picking is off in cinematic mode.
- OrbitTrail: for the selected asteroid a 480-point Earth-relative path from 200 days before to 400 days after the
  clock, through the same compressed mapping, fading dim to bright; rebuilt every 110 simulated days or when the
  selection changes.
- Memory for 42,670 objects: element table 4.1 MB, positions 0.5 MB x 2, instance matrices 2.7 MB.

2.11 Cinematic mode, step by step
---------------------------------
1. "Cinematic" on the InfoCard calls startCinema(index) (lib/cinematic/controller.ts). It cancels any earlier job
   (a run token discards stale callbacks), sets store.cinema = "loading" with stage "ephemeris" (HUD text: loading
   the planet and Moon tables, about 15 MB, cached after the first time).
2. It awaits ready() (the NBodyClient worker has fetched manifest.json, planets.bin and moon.bin, with a
   ?v=count-count-step cache-buster, and built the Ephemeris) and loadMainEphemeris() (a second copy on the main
   thread for planet positions in the solar-system view). The coverage [start, end] is published (lib/coverage.ts)
   and bounds the date picker.
3. Start time = the dashboard clock converted to TDB, clamped to [coverage start + 0.5 d, coverage end - 30 d]; a
   note is shown if it moved. Stage becomes "integrating".
4. NBodyClient.run(elements from asteroids.json, tStart, {direction: 1, chunkSamples: 1500}, {includeMoon: true})
   posts a job to nbody/nbody.worker.ts, which iterates the simulateStream generator (1.14-1.17). Per chunk it
   converts samples to Float32 arrays (samplesToArrays: geocentric position, heliocentric position, Moon geocentric
   position, distances to Earth and Moon), posts them with a transfer list, and yields to the event loop
   (setTimeout 0) so a cancel message can arrive. Events (close approaches, Hill crossings, impact) ride with each
   chunk; the final summary ends the job.
5. appendChunk on the main thread: typed arrays grow by doubling, the closest approach so far is tracked, and the
   two scene polylines are extended (localPath = cinemaOffsetToScene of geocentric positions, widePath = toWide of
   heliocentric ones); pathVersion tells the scene to re-upload them. With 2 samples in hand, begin() sets
   cine.active = true: PLAYBACK STARTS WHILE THE INTEGRATOR STILL RUNS. The HUD shows "Integrating ... N%" and the
   scrub range grows with the samples that exist.
6. Every frame (scene/Cinematic.tsx CinematicDriver):
   a. sampleAt (cinematic/trajectoryMath.ts): binary search for the sample interval containing cine.t, LINEAR
      interpolation of position between samples, speed from the interval. Sampling is dense near Earth (1 min) and
      sparse far away (1 d), so linear interpolation is adequate where it matters.
   b. Time warp. Base rate cine.baseRate (default 60 simulated days per real second; menu 5/20/60/180/365). With
      "Slow near approach" on:
          rate = min(baseRate, max(tau / 4, 0.002)),       tau = distance / speed   (days to cover the distance)
      so the remaining approach or departure never takes less than ~4 real seconds, with a floor of 0.002 d/s
      (2.9 simulated minutes per second). Example: 0.1 AU at 7 km/s gives tau = 24.7 d, rate = 6.2 d/s.
   c. cine.t += rate * dt (dt capped at 50 ms). Stop rules: if t reaches the end of what exists, wait for more
      samples; if the run is complete with status window_end (reached 2100-12-31) LOOP to the start; if the
      status is impact_earth, impact_moon or escaped, stop exactly at tStop, set ended and show the banner (impact
      adds an expanding additive flash sphere; escaped says the object leaves and does not return). Replay
      restarts; the slider scrubs anywhere in [tStart, tStop].
   d. View weight w (0 = Earth-centred, 1 = solar system). Auto: w_target = smoothstep(0.02, 0.25, distance in AU),
      so inside 0.02 AU the view is Earth-centred and beyond 0.25 AU heliocentric; w eases toward it with
      1 - exp(-2.2 dt). Earth sits at toWide(Earth heliocentric from the tables); the asteroid blends between
      Earth + cinemaOffsetToScene(geocentric) and toWide(heliocentric); the Moon between its true-scale offset
      and a fixed 2.1-unit orbit; the Sun lies far along the true Sun direction in Earth view and at the origin in
      the wide view; Mercury..Neptune are placed from the tables (analytic ellipses for the orbit lines); Earth
      and Moon shrink (x0.3 and x0.45) in the wide view.
7. Scene changes: the directional light is replaced by a point light at the Sun; AsteroidField is hidden (only the
   selected asteroid is shown); AsteroidActor draws the rock, a ring, past and future trails (Earth-relative trail
   limited to +-45 days, heliocentric trail whole); CinematicCamera frames Earth plus asteroid (capped at 40 units of
   separation) or, in wide view, at least 120 units out, blending with the same w; it keeps the user's orbit
   direction and zoom ratio, drifts when the user is idle for 3.5 s, zoom limits 0.5..900.
8. exitCinema() cancels the job, writes the cinematic time back into the dashboard clock (clamped) and returns home.

2.12 Known accuracy limits and caveats
--------------------------------------
Apophis in numbers (1.19): two-body 0.000138 AU; N-body from full-precision elements 0.000254102 AU (Horizons
0.000254091, 1.7 km, 0.2 min); N-body from the rounded elements shipped in asteroids.json 0.000246714 AU (1,105 km
off); the engine-vs-Horizons gap one year after the flyby is 14,231 km (9.5e-5 AU), and 5.7e-4 AU after ~7.6 years.
 1. Rounded elements. asteroids.json holds a, e to 6 decimals and angles to 4. Fine for dots, but cinematic mode
    integrates from these values, so sensitive flybys move (1 km at the start becomes 47,656 km by 2030 for
    Apophis). Elements carry no uncertainty (SBDB covariance is not used): results are "what these elements
    imply", not forecasts with error bars. Elements rounded to 3-4 digits would be useless (0.009619 AU for Apophis).
 2. Not modelled in N-body: massive asteroids (JPL's Apophis solution uses 16), Pluto, Yarkovsky and radiation
    forces, Earth J2 and tides, solar J2, planetary 1PN. test_nbody.mts attributes the post-flyby Horizons gap to
    Yarkovsky plus the 16 asteroids, amplified by the flyby.
 3. Old epochs. 5,297 objects have element epochs before the tables begin; their N-body start is two-body-propagated
    (2.7).
 4. Two-body screening. Flyby dates and distances after ~2040 are indicative only: the osculating a differs from the
    long-term mean by ~1e-4..1e-3 relative, giving up to ~0.7 rad of phase error over 74 years; encounters change
    orbits (Apophis 2029: a from 0.92 to ~1.1 AU, which two-body cannot know); Earth's focusing is ignored (matters
    below ~0.001 AU); the model's own Apophis passes in 2060 and 2091 differ from JPL's 2051 and 2066.
 5. "impact" is not a prediction. 12,314 objects (28.9%) carry the label; it is mostly orbit geometry (a conjunction
    near a node crossing within 74 years). Read it with min_dist_au, min_date, strict_collision (16 objects) and
    next_close_approach. Even strict_collision is an unperturbed, unfocused geometry statement.
 6. Diameters. 97% are estimated from H with albedo 0.14; true albedos run from ~0.03 to ~0.5, so a factor ~2 or more
    either way (Apophis: 0.54 km estimated versus 0.34 km measured).
 7. Dashboard Earth is the Standish Earth-Moon barycentre, up to 1.4e-4 AU (~21,000 km) from DE441's Earth centre.
    InfoCard "Distance now" and the dots use it; min_dist_au in the file uses DE441. At the instant of 1.19 (6) the
    two give 0.000263 versus 0.000188 AU for Apophis. The dashboard Moon is a circular mean-longitude model on the
    compressed scale (visual only); cinematic mode uses the true Horizons Moon.
 8. Ephemeris coverage. 2020-01-01..2100-12-31 (extended 2026-10-09; previously to 2036-12-31). Cinematic start is
    clamped into [start + 0.5 d, end - 30 d]; runs end at 2100-12-31; the dashboard clock runs [today, 2100-12-31].
    15.6 MB of tables are downloaded (and held twice: worker and main thread); the browser caches them.
 9. Interpolation error is 0.1 km (Earth), 20 km (Mercury), 0.3 km (Moon): far below anything visible.
10. Time scale: the dashboard ignores the 69 s UTC/TDB offset (1.16).
11. Two Earth radii exist in code: 6371 km (nbody/constants.ts, used for impact tests) and EARTH_RADIUS_AU =
    4.2635e-5 AU = 6378 km (kepler.ts, used for the display scale); 0.1% apart.
12. UI text. ControlsPanel prints "Categories are computed for the <today> to <coverage end> window" using today's
    date, while the pipeline window starts on the fixed date 2026-10-09; the label is exact only on that day.
13. Planet fallback. cinematic/ephemeris.ts PLANETS has Earth's mean-longitude rate 35999.37306329 while kepler.ts
    uses Table 1's 35999.37244981; the difference (6e-6 deg per century) is immaterial and affects only the fallback
    and the orbit lines. I did not check which JPL table the first value comes from.
14. No WebGL, or a scene exception: App.tsx shows a static Earth disc (supportsWebGL, ErrorBoundary).

3.5 The "backend": offline scripts plus static files (no server, no database)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
Nothing runs on a server. All data work happens beforehand on a developer machine (Python 3.12 + numpy; no other packages,
no requirements file) and writes static files that git stores and a static host serves. The browser does the rest. Network
is needed only when running the scripts: JPL SBDB (query API and, for aliases, the single-object API) and JPL Horizons
(plus the DE440 header file). Run from the repo root with `python -I` (isolated mode: ignores PYTHON* variables and user
site-packages). Order:
    1. python -I scripts/fetch_sbdb.py
           -> data/raw/sbdb_neo.json                                     (SBDB; exit 2 on network failure)
    2. python -I scripts/build_elements.py                               (needs neo_v2.csv and step 1)
           -> data/neo_elements.json, data/coverage_report.md, data/raw/match_log.json, data/raw/sbdb_alias_resolve.json
    3. python -I scripts/validate.py                                     checks; exit 1 on failure
    4. python -I scripts/fetch_ephemeris.py --validate                   (Horizons; full 2020-01-01..2100-12-31 fetch)
           -> app/public/data/ephemeris/{planets.bin, moon.bin, manifest.json}
           other flags: --extend (only the missing span), --validate-only, --moon-only, --workers N, --cache-dir DIR,
           --start-jd J, --days N, --moon-hours H (must divide 24)
    5. python -I scripts/trajectory.py                                   (needs step 2; uses the step-4 tables for Earth if present)
           -> app/public/data/asteroids.json, data/asteroids_trajectories.json, data/trajectory_report.md
    6. python -I scripts/validate.py                                     again: now also checks the step-5 outputs
    7. python -I scripts/test_trajectory.py
    8. node scripts/test_nbody.mts
When to repeat what: steps 1-2 refresh the catalogue; step 5 and the tests follow any change to elements or ephemeris; step 4
only when the date range or Moon step changes. The analysis window is hard-coded in trajectory.py (START_JD = 2026-10-09,
END_JD = 2100-12-31): re-running on a later day does not move the start unless those constants are edited.
App: cd app; npm install; npm run dev (Vite default http://localhost:5173; .claude/launch.json uses port 5174); npm run build;
npm run preview; npm run gen:mock [count] regenerates the placeholder dataset.

3.8 Glossary
------------
AU            astronomical unit, 149,597,870.7 km, about Earth-Sun distance.
LD            lunar distance, 384,399 km = 0.00256955 AU.
Ecliptic      plane of Earth's orbit around the Sun; J2000 = the frame fixed at the year-2000 equinox.
Julian Date   continuous day count; JD 2451545.0 = 2000-01-01 12:00.
UTC, TT, TDB  civil time; terrestrial time (UTC + 69.184 s now); barycentric dynamical time (TT within 2 ms), used by JPL.
Osculating    "kissing": the Kepler ellipse that matches position and velocity at one instant (the epoch).
Elements      a, e, i, Om, w, M0 (1.2). Perihelion/aphelion: closest/farthest point from the Sun.
Mean, eccentric, true anomaly   three angles locating a body on its orbit: uniform clock angle, auxiliary-circle angle, real angle.
Kepler's equation   M = E - e sin E, links mean and eccentric anomaly. Newton-Raphson/Halley: iterative root finders.
NEO           near-Earth object: perihelion below 1.3 AU. PHA: potentially hazardous asteroid (MOID <= 0.05 AU and H <= 22,
              flagged by JPL). MOID: minimum orbit intersection distance (1.9).
H, albedo     absolute magnitude (brightness at standard geometry); fraction of light reflected. D = 1329/sqrt(p) * 10^(-H/5).
SBDB          JPL Small-Body Database (orbits, sizes). Horizons: JPL ephemeris service (positions of planets, moons, asteroids).
DE440/DE441   JPL planetary ephemerides (numerical models of the solar system).
Barycentre    centre of mass; EMB = Earth-Moon barycentre (~4,671 km from Earth's centre).
Hill sphere   region where a planet's gravity dominates the Sun's; ~0.01 AU for Earth.
Close approach, flyby, perigee    time and distance of least separation from Earth; perigee measured to Earth's centre.
Gravitational focusing   Earth's pull bends a passing body inward, so the true perigee is smaller than the straight-line miss.
Two-body model   only Sun and asteroid considered (a Kepler ellipse). N-body: all significant masses included. Point mass: a
              body treated as having no size or shape. 1PN: first post-Newtonian relativistic correction.
Yarkovsky     tiny thrust from anisotropic thermal re-radiation of sunlight, which slowly changes an asteroid's orbit.
IAS15, Gauss-Radau   the integrator and the node set it uses (1.14). Kahan summation: error-compensated addition.
Hermite interpolation   cubic curve through two points matching their velocities (1.13).
Instanced mesh   one GPU draw call for many copies of a shape. Web worker: a background thread. Transferable: a buffer handed to
              another thread without copying. Double buffering: two buffers alternate between writer and reader.
Typed array   fixed-type numeric array (Float32Array, Float64Array) for fast numeric work in JavaScript.
Time warp     slowing playback automatically when something interesting happens (2.11).
